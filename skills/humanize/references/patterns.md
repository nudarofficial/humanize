## Content

- Generic praise in place of a specific fact. Keep the fact.
- Inflated importance and legacy: stands/serves as, is a testament/reminder, a crucial/pivotal/vital/significant/key role or moment, underscores/highlights its importance, reflects broader, symbolizing its ongoing/enduring/lasting, contributing to the, setting the stage for, marking/shaping the, represents/marks a shift, key turning point, evolving landscape, focal point, indelible mark, deeply rooted, forms part of the wider, "this distinction matters because". It turns up even for etymology or population data. For plants and animals it becomes long asides on ecosystems and conservation status, and sometimes it follows an admission that the topic is minor. Since mid-2026 it often editorializes that a fact's appearance in a source proves importance ("its continued appearance in itineraries reflects the importance of the site").
- Claims that a topic "has shaped conversations/discussions/debate about" something else, "generated debate about" or "prompted broader reflection on", often with three topics listed.
- Notability padding: naming outlets and labelling their type (independent coverage, regional/national media outlets, music/business/tech outlets, trade publications, cited/featured/profiled in) without saying what the coverage said. In bios and pitches, name the outlet and what it reported, or leave it out.
- Stressing how sources identify the subject: "databases/records/listings identify him as", "was identified by [source] as", "is listed in", "her professional profile lists". State the fact and put the source in the citation.
- "Maintains an active/strong digital/social media presence."
- Shallow "-ing" tails on factual sentences: highlighting/underscoring/emphasizing..., ensuring..., reflecting/symbolizing..., contributing to..., cultivating/fostering..., encompassing..., enhancing..., plus "valuable insights" and "align/resonate with". Newer models pin these readings on a named person who never said them ("Roger Ebert highlighted the lasting influence").
- Vague connection: in connection with/to, connected with/to, in association with, associated with ("particularly/widely associated"), and roundabout career verbs ("ventured into politics as a candidate", "began his career as") where "was" works.
- Weasel attribution and inflated counts: industry reports, observers have cited, experts argue, some critics argue, "several sources/publications" when one or two exist, "such as" before a list that's actually complete, "is described in scholarship as".
- The challenges formula: "Despite its [praise], X faces several challenges...", "Despite these challenges", ending on vague optimism or speculation about future initiatives, plus "Challenges and legacy" or "Future outlook" sections. Mentioning a real challenge is fine; the rigid template is the tell.
- "X and Y" headings, especially "Awards and recognition" (or just "Recognition").

## Language

- "AI vocabulary" in clusters. One or two can be chance; a cluster is one of the strongest tells. The page tracks the words by period:
  - additionally (especially opening a sentence), boasts, bolstered, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, garner, intricate/intricacies, interplay, key, landscape (abstract), meticulously, pivotal, underscore, tapestry (abstract), testament, valuable, vibrant (2023 to mid-2024)
  - align with, bolstered, crucial, emphasizing, enhance, enduring, fostering, highlighting, pivotal, showcasing, underscore, vibrant (mid-2024 to mid-2025)
  - emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing (mid-2025 to mid-2026)
  - challenge, clearer, dependable, echoed, foster, leverage, matters, multifaceted, practical, prioritize, quietly, steady, universally (mid-2026 on)
  - causal, empirical, correlate, underscore (Grok); the general list adds deep dive, highlight as a verb, meticulous and robust
  Context counts: "underscore" can be a literal underline, and "landscape" can be a photo of hills. Everyday 2026 words like "challenge" or "practical" only signal anything in clusters.
- Dodging "is" and "has": serves as, stands as, marks, functions as, operates as, represents, boasts, features, maintains, offers, refers to, "holds the distinction of being". AI copyedits often "improve" plain sentences this way.
- Negative parallelisms: "not only X but also Y", "it's not just X, it's Y", "it's not X, it's Y", "no X, no Y, just Z", and the reversed "Y rather than X" that 2026 output overuses. A plain "but" is fine. The tell is the corrective frame that implies the reader had it wrong, or the same balanced "praised X but criticized Y" in every sentence.
- Defining a list or broad topic as if it were a thing ("Historic churches of Lamont County refers to...").
- Rule of three: adjective triplets, three short phrases in a row, "X, Y and Z" used to make thin analysis look complete. It stands out most where nobody would bother with flourishes, like a quick message or an edit summary.
- 2026 rhythm: heavy use of periods and commas, with sentences held between about 8 and 20 words.

## Style and formatting

- Title case in headings.
- Bold scattered through prose, or raw double asterisks where bold doesn't render.
- Lists whose items open with a bold label and a colon, and typed bullet characters or emoji where real list formatting belongs.
- Heading errors: a title heading that repeats the document name, skipped levels, several top-level headings, a heading with nothing under it except more headings.
- Emoji at the start of headings or bullets.
- Curly quotes and apostrophes, or a mix of curly and straight.
- Small tables that would read better as a sentence.
- Horizontal rules between sections.

## Talking to the user inside the deliverable

- Chatbot replies left in the text: "a Wikipedia-style article", "I hope this helps", "Of course!", "Certainly!", "You're absolutely right!", "Would you like...", "is there anything else", "let me know", "more detailed breakdown", "here is a...", "check/verify/confirm ... before publishing or submitting". Also hidden notes or advice to the user left inside a draft.
- Source-availability hedges: "while specific details are limited/scarce", "not widely available/documented/disclosed", "in the provided/available sources" or "search results", "based on available information", "maintains a low profile", "keeps personal details private". These are usually guesses dressed up as caution. Find the information or leave the point out.
- Older didactic asides: "it's important to note", and safety or "varies by jurisdiction" disclaimers nobody asked for.
- Corrective framings, new in mid-2026: "[claim] should be treated/presented as X rather than Y", "this does not by itself establish...", and (a weaker sign) "should not be confused with".
- Unfilled templates: [Name], [Year], [Founder Names], [Entertainer's Name], "(Add your channel URL here)", "(If available)", 2025-XX-XX dates, example.com/source1, INSERT_SOURCE_URL.
- Knowledge-cutoff lines ("as of my last knowledge update") and refusals ("As an AI language model...", "I'm sorry, but... I can...").
- Text that stops mid-sentence.

## Sources, citations and links

- Invented references are the most serious item on this list. Never produce a source, quote, DOI, ISBN, page number or URL you haven't verified.
- Dead links that were never archived, DOIs that don't resolve or lead to an unrelated paper, ISBNs with bad checksums, book citations without page numbers (or with pages that don't support the claim), and access dates that predate the writing or are placeholders.
- Tracking tags in URLs: utm_source=chatgpt.com, utm_source=openai, utm_source=copilot.com, referrer=grok.com. Strip them.
- Chatbot citation code: `contentReference[oaicite:0]`, `oai_citation`, `turn0search0`, `+1`, `[cite: 1]`, `[span_1](start_span)`, `<mark>` tags, `grok_card`, `【85†L261-269】`, `[attached_file:1]`, `[web:1]`, `ppl-ai-file-upload`, `:::writing{variant="document"}`, and ↩ arrows around footnotes.
- Reference lists with entries the text never uses, or broken reuse of the same reference.

## Messages, replies and change notes

- Customer-service voice: "I have carefully reviewed", "I apologize for any inconvenience I may have caused", "If there are specific issues, please point them out to me and I will resolve them".
- When answering criticism: claiming human effort to play down AI use, asking critics to list exactly what to fix, dismissing concerns as "unsubstantiated speculation", telling critics to focus on content instead.
- Splitting a message into titled sections when a few sentences would do, and citing rules or policies that don't exist.
- Canned change notes: refined, enhanced, enriched, streamlined, improved clarity/flow/readability, ensured neutrality, "in compliance with", "while preserving the original meaning", "added sourced/verified content", "addressed reviewer feedback", lists of template or parameter names. Say what changed, briefly and specifically.

## Hooks in social copy

A list posted on the page's talk page adds these, and they're common in AI-written posts: "Here's the thing", "The truth is", colon reveals ("Here's the best part:") and faux insight ("This is the part nobody tells you"). Open with the actual claim or question.

## Older signs (rarer now, still avoid)

- Brochure promotion: boasts a, vibrant, rich, profound, enhancing, showcasing, exemplifies, commitment to, natural beauty, nestled, in the heart of, groundbreaking, renowned, featuring, diverse array.
- Section summaries and "Conclusion" sections ("In summary", "In conclusion", "Overall", "To sum up").
- Em dashes, especially spaced ones used for punch. This skill avoids them entirely.
- Elegant variation: a new label for the same thing in each sentence ("the company", "the firm", "the tech giant", "the innovator").

## Not reliable signs (don't over-correct)

The page lists these as weak or misleading signals: perfect grammar, a mix of casual and formal registers, "bland" or "robotic" prose, formal or academic words in general, transition words on their own, missing citations, odd wikitext glitches and correct wikitext. Don't make writing worse to avoid them. Good grammar stays, and so do transitions where they help a reader follow along.

## Wikipedia only

This applies only if the user edits Wikipedia. According to WikiProject AI Cleanup, the WP:LLM guideline has banned LLM-generated or LLM-rewritten article content since March 2026, apart from translation and basic copyediting. If the user asks for article text, point that out first; help with finding sources or understanding policy instead. The Wikipedia-specific signs are Markdown in place of wikitext (asterisks for bold, number signs for headings, dashed dividers, `[text](url)` links, code blocks marked "wikitext"), broken wikitext, whole articles wrapped in `<nowiki>` tags, categories and magic words missing their colon prefix, categories and templates that don't exist, maintenance templates or a "declined" AfC template already placed on a new draft, AfC "submission statements" addressed to reviewers, AI-style edit summaries, and canned user pages ("About Me", "My Interests", "Let's Connect!").
